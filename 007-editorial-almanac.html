The Almanac of Small Weathers

For ninety-one years, one family has kept a record of the weather nobody else bothered to write down.

By Ottilie Marchbank · Fourteen-minute read
❦

There is a ledger on a shelf above the stove in the Penhallow farmhouse, and it is not about rain. Rain has its own instruments, its own government offices, its own radio voice at six in the morning. The ledger is about the other weathers: the hour the frost lets go of the gate latch, the first evening the swifts stop screaming over the barn, the week the kitchen smells of wet wool for no reason anyone can name.

On the LedgerEleven volumes, bound in oxblood calfskin by a saddler in Wethercombe. The first entry is dated 3 March 1934.

Agnes Penhallow began it the spring her husband went north for work. She was, by her own account in the margin of page one, “lonely enough to talk to the weather and sensible enough to write down what it said back.” Her daughter kept it after her, then a grandson who had wanted to be a sailor and settled for the next best thing, which was to watch the sky from a hill two hundred miles from any sea.

I · MethodWhat counts as a weather

The rules, such as they are, were never written down in one place. They have to be inferred, the way you learn the rules of a card game by losing at it. A weather, in the Penhallow sense, is anything that changes the mood of the house and arrives from outside it. A fox crossing the lower field at noon is a weather. A letter is not. The church bell sounding flat on a humid evening is a weather. The bell itself is not.

Margin, 1951“Heard the bell go sour. Thunder by supper. Bell knew first.”

What emerges, across ninety-one years and some forty thousand entries, is something like a second climate. It runs alongside the official one and occasionally contradicts it. The official record says the summer of 1976 was a drought. The ledger says it was “the summer the dog slept in the dairy,” and lists, with real tenderness, every cool stone floor in the parish.

“Lonely enough to talk to the weather, and sensible enough to write down what it said back.”Agnes Penhallow, page one

II · MeasuresA calendar kept by hand

Leafing through the volumes, certain dates surface again and again, each one marked in the same hand-drawn symbols that Agnes invented and nobody since has dared to change: a small crescent for a first frost, a hook for the swifts, a closed eye for fog thick enough that the hens refuse to leave the coop.

The drift in the first frost is the line that brought meteorologists to the farmhouse door. Eleven days across a single lifetime is not a statistic you can argue with when it is written in three different hands, in ink and pencil and once in what appears to be blackberry juice.

III · InheritanceWho keeps it now

Wren Penhallow is twenty-six and came home from the city to look after the ledger the way some people come home to look after a parent. She writes in it each night at the kitchen table, under the same lamp, in a fountain pen she admits is mostly for ceremony.

Wren, 2024“Pylons humming. First time in my life I've noticed. Gran would have.”

She has added one rule of her own. Each autumn, on the evening of the first frost, she reads aloud the entry from the same date fifty years earlier. Then she steps outside and checks whether the gate latch is cold enough to hold her fingertip. Some years it is. More often, now, it is not, and she comes back inside and writes that down too.

“People ask if it's sad,” she says, closing the eleventh volume and setting the pen across it like a bookmark. “It isn't. It's just attention. The weather has always been talking. Somebody in this house has always been listening. I'd hate to be the one who stopped.”
